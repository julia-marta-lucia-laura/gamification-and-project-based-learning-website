Article 1

BENEFITS OF GAMIFIED LEARNING IN PHYSICAL EDUCATION STUDENTS

Ferriz-Valero, Agulló-Pomares i Tortosa-Martínez (2023)

Summary of the article: Introduction
  • Physical Education (PE) aims to develop students' motor, cognitive, social and emotional skills and promote an active lifestyle.
  • Games are already widely used in PE as teaching resources.
  • Gamification: The application of game elements and principles to existing learning environments to increase motivation and engagement.
  • Unlike:
    • Serious games: Games that prioritise educational objectives over entertainment.
    • Game-based learning: Using games as a resource to support learning.

Gamification transforms an existing learning process into a game-like experience.

Main benefits of gamification
  • Motivation: Most studies reported increased motivation, including both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.
  • Learning & academic performance: Gamification improves learning (ICT skills, healthy habits, knowledge and life skills) and student satisfaction.
  • Engagement: Greater commitment to learning, participation and physical activity.
  • Teamwork: Improved cooperation and relationships among students.
  • Classroom climate: Greater classroom cohesion, a more positive learning environment, reduced violent behaviours and development of social and life skills.
  • Basic psychological needs: Some studies found improvements in AUTONOMY, COMPETENCE and RELATION
Other observed outcomes
  • Improvements in perceived and comparative motor competence, as well as healthy lifestyle habits.
  • Differences in emotional responses according to sex.
  • Students generally evaluated gamification positively, although teachers reported an increased workload.
How does gamification influence learning?

According to the article, gamification can influence learning through two processes:

  • Mediating process (more direct)
  • Moderating process (more indirect)
KEY ELEMENTS OF GAMIFIED INTERVENTIONS

Gamification models: PBL vs. MDA

PBL (Points, Badges and Leaderboards):A simpler, more superficial approach based on points, badges and rankings.
MDA (Mechanics, Dynamics and Aesthetics):A deeper and more complex approach combining different game elements.

Both models showed positive results; there is insufficient evidence to determine which is more effective in PE.

Narrative

  • A game-fiction component used in many gamified interventions.

However, the review suggests that narrative is not a determining factor in increasing motivation in PE.

Feedback and rewards

  • Continuous feedback helps students monitor their progress through points, badges, levels, progress bars and leaderboards.
  • Public rankings may encourage excessive competitiveness and uncomfortable comparisons.
  • Rewards should be used carefully, as they may reduce intrinsic motivation among already motivated students.
Conclusions
  • Gamification can improve students’ motivation, engagement, autonomy, teamwork and learning in PE.
  • Its effectiveness depends on how well game elements work together, rather than simply including as many elements as possible. Gamification alone does not guarantee learning.
  • Students’ voluntary participation and interest in the learning content are also important.
  • The results should be interpreted with caution due to the methodological limitations of the studies, and further research is needed to confirm the effectiveness of gamification in PE.

Article 2

GAMIFICATION IN PHYSICAL EDUCATION: A SYSTEMATIC REVIEW

Víctor Arufe Giráldez, Alberto Sanmiguel Rodríguez, Oliver Ramos Álvarez and Rubén Navarro Patón (2022)

Summary of the article: Gamification

Gamification is an active teaching method that uses elements from games and video games in lessons to make them more fun and motivating. Teachers use resources such as points, badges, levels, rewards, challenges, and rankings. The goal is to encourage students to participate more, become more involved in activities, and enjoy the learning process. In Physical Education, gamification helps promote physical activity and makes classes more attractive. Gamification needs to be well-designed and planned.

Aim of the study

The aim of this article was to review all research published up to 2021 about gamification in Physical Education. The researchers wanted to find out whether this method really helps improve students' motivation, participation, learning, academic performance, and physical activity habits.

Methodology

To carry out the study, the authors followed the PRISMA model, which is a method used to conduct scientific reviews in a rigorous way. They searched for information in five international databases and initially found 177 articles. After removing duplicate articles and those that did not meet the requirements, they finally selected 17 studies for analysis.

Articles found177
Studies analysed17
Studies found

Of the 17 studies selected:

  • None were from Early Childhood Education.
  • 7 were from Primary Education.
  • 10 were from Secondary Education.
Early Childhood Education0
Primary Education7
Secondary Education10

This shows that there is still very little research on gamification in Physical Education, especially in Early Childhood Education.

Results in Primary Education

In Primary Education, most studies showed positive results. Students were more motivated, participated more, and had a more positive attitude towards Physical Education classes. Improvements were also found in teamwork, autonomy, and relationships between classmates.

Some studies reported better learning of the content and a greater interest in doing physical activity. Students also said that classes were more enjoyable and entertaining when gamification was used.

Results in Secondary Education

In Secondary Education, the results were also very positive. Students showed higher motivation, greater interest in activities, and more participation in class. Many students became more committed to their tasks and enjoyed the subject more.

Some programs also helped reduce sedentary behaviour among teenagers and increased their physical activity levels. In addition, improvements were observed in physical fitness aspects such as endurance, agility, and speed.

Hybrid models

Several studies combined gamification with other active teaching methods, such as cooperative learning, the flipped classroom, or the personal and social responsibility model.

The results suggest that these combinations can have positive effects as they encourage motivation, responsibility, autonomy, and student participation.

Most common gamification elements

The elements most frequently used in the studies were:

  • Points
  • Badges
  • Levels
  • Rewards
  • Challenges
  • Rankings

However, the authors explain that many experiences did not use all the elements needed to create a complete gamification process.

Main benefits

The benefits most commonly reported in the studies were:

  • Increased motivation
  • Greater participation in class
  • More involvement in activities
  • Better relationships between classmates
  • More autonomy
  • Improved physical fitness
  • Reduced sedentary behaviour
  • A more positive attitude towards physical activity

Overall, most results indicate that gamification has positive effects on students.

Limitations of the study

The authors also highlighted some limitations. The number of studies is still quite small, and many of them use different methodologies, which makes it difficult to compare results. In addition, some studies have small sample sizes and do not include control groups.

Because of this, it was not possible to carry out a meta-analysis using all the collected data.

Conclusions

Gamification is a teaching method with great potential in Physical Education. Most studies show that it helps increase students' motivation, participation, and engagement in physical activity. It can also contribute to improving physical fitness, autonomy, and some cognitive skills.

However, researchers believe that more studies are needed, especially those using more rigorous methodologies, to confirm all these benefits. They also highlight the need for more research on gamification in Early Childhood Education, as there are currently almost no studies in this area.

Article 3

GAME-BASED LEARNING AND GAMIFICATION IN PHYSICAL EDUCATION: A SYSTEMATIC REVIEW

Rubén Camacho Sánchez, Ana Manzano León, José Miguel Rodríguez Ferrer, Jorge Serna and Pere Lavega Burgués (2023)

Summary of the article: Why use games in PE?

Games can make learning more active, meaningful and motivating. In PE, they can promote:

  • Cooperation and socialisation
  • Active participation and decision-making
  • Technical, tactical and theoretical learning
  • Enjoyment

→ Games are not only for practising physical skills, they can also be a learning resource.

GBL vs Gamification
Game-Based Learning (GBL)→ students play a game to learn a specific concept.
  • Serious games: designed specifically for learning.
  • Exergames: video games involving physical activity.
Gamification→ game elements are added to a non-game learning context (points, badges, levels, challenges, etc.).

→ Both can improve motivation and engagement.

Main gamification elements

There are 3 main categories:

  • Dynamics: narrative, progression, relationships.
  • Mechanics: rules, challenges, cooperation, coordination.
  • Components: points, badges, leaderboards, levels.

Their combination can increase motivation and performance, although research results are not always consistent.

Main results of the review

Most studies were conducted in:

Secondary (47%) → Primary (29%) → University (24%)

Secondary47%
Primary29%
University24%

Most common elements:

  • Narratives and challenges → 47%
  • Collaboration + competition → 80%
  • Points/rewards/leaderboards → 76%
  • Technology was used in 86% of the studies.
Narratives and challenges47%
Collaboration + competition80%
Points/rewards/leaderboards76%
Technology86%

Main effects:

  • ↑ Motivation
  • ↑ Autonomy, competence and relatedness
  • Possible improvements in academic performance and health
  • Physical performance → mixed results.
Technology

Technology can support motivation, socialisation and learning, but:

Technology ≠ motivation automatically

→ It needs to have a meaningful educational purpose, not be used just because it is attractive or new

Key idea: Context + Design

GBL and gamification can be beneficial, but they do not work automatically.

Teachers need to:
analyse the context → consider the students → adapt/design the game accordingly.

→ The same strategy can have different results depending on how and where it is implemented.

Conclusion

GBL and gamification can benefit motivation, learning, health, motor control and psychological needs in PE.

They can be used at all educational levels, including university, but their effectiveness depends mainly on the context, educational level and design.

Article 1

A review of project-based learning in higher education: Student outcomes and measures

Guo, Saab, Post & Admiraal (2020) · International Journal of Educational Research, 102 · DOI

What is the article about?

The article looks at project-based learning (PBL) in universities. In PBL, students work on real projects and make a final product, such as a website, a report or a machine. The authors want to know what students learn from PBL and how researchers measure it.

Why is this study important?

Universities want students to have hard skills (knowledge and professional skills) and soft skills (like teamwork and problem-solving). Traditional classes often don’t help with this, because the teacher talks and the students only listen. PBL can help, but most research on it is about schools, not universities.

Research questions
  • What student outcomes of PBL are studied in higher education?
  • What instruments do researchers use to measure these outcomes?
Method

The authors searched in several academic databases and found 450 articles. After checking them with clear rules (for example, the study had to be empirical and had to include a final product), they chose 76 articles for the review.

Articles found450
Articles chosen76
Main results

The authors found four types of outcomes:

  • Cognitive outcomes: knowledge (17 studies) and learning strategies (9 studies).
  • Affective outcomes (feelings and opinions): what students think they gained from PBL (37 studies) and how they felt about the experience (31 studies). This was the most common type.
  • Behavioral outcomes: skills (9 studies) and engagement (4 studies).
  • Artifact performance: the quality of the final product (10 studies). All products were evaluated with rubrics.
Affective: gains37
Affective: experience31
Cognitive: knowledge17
Artifact performance10
Cognitive: strategies9
Behavioral: skills9
Behavioral: engagement4

The most common instrument was the questionnaire. Researchers also used tests, rubrics, interviews, observation, reflection journals, artifacts and log data.

Problems the authors found
  • Many studies did not describe their questionnaires clearly or did not show if they were reliable and valid.
  • Some studies with qualitative data did not check the quality of their analysis.
  • Most studies had only one group and no comparison group, so we cannot be sure that PBL caused the results.
  • Few studies looked at the learning process or at the final products, even though making a product is the main feature of PBL.
  • Most studies were in STEM subjects. There are few in humanities and social sciences.
Recommendations for future research
  • Study more how students learn during the project and how good their final products are.
  • Use log data from computers to understand student behavior better.
  • Explain the instruments clearly and report their reliability and validity.
  • Do more experimental research to measure the real effects of PBL.
  • Try PBL in more subjects, not only STEM.
Conclusion

PBL seems to be a promising way to teach in higher education, and the authors encourage teachers to use it. However, we need better research, with better instruments and designs, to understand its real effects.

Article 2

Project-Based Learning and Problem-Based Learning: Are They Effective to Improve Student’s Thinking Skills?

R. D. Anazifa & Djukri, Universitas Negeri Yogyakarta, Indonesia · Jurnal Pendidikan IPA Indonesia, 6(2), 2017, pp. 346-355 · DOI

Summary: Introduction

The study asks two questions:

  1. Do project-based learning (PjBL) and problem-based learning (PBL) improve students’ creativity and critical thinking?
  2. Does one of the two models work better than the other?

It was carried out in high school biology lessons on the respiratory system.

Why the study was done
  • Modern education needs new skills. Students should learn not only content but also creativity, critical thinking, problem solving, communication, and collaboration (known as “21st-century skills”).
  • Indonesia’s curriculum asks for it. The 2013 Curriculum recommends PjBL, PBL, discovery learning, and guided inquiry to develop these skills.
  • The problem at the school. PjBL and PBL were rarely used in biology. Students showed low creativity (they completed tasks in very similar ways) and weak analytical skills when answering questions.
  • Two key ideas behind the study:
    • Creativity can be learned with continuous practice, and learning through projects is one of the most effective ways to develop it.
    • Critical thinking means interpreting information, analysing, making inferences, evaluating, and explaining. It is a higher-order skill, and open-ended questions and real problems help to develop it.
  • Why the respiratory system? The school is in Temanggung, a major tobacco-growing region, so smoking and lung health are a real and familiar issue for students.
The two learning models
FeatureProject-based learning (PjBL)Problem-based learning (PBL)
Main ideaStudents learn by building a project or product that answers a real-world questionStudents learn by investigating a real, open-ended problem that has more than one possible solution
Starting pointA broad driving question that students narrow down, plus an anchor (a journal and an article) that introduces the projectA problem with limited information, so students must search for more
TaskStructured and set by the teacherOpen-ended
KnowledgePracticalOpen-ended
Students’ roleComplete the project as team membersActive participants who direct their own learning
Teacher’s roleSets tasks and supervises the projectCreates opportunities to learn and guides discussion
Main focusSolving problems and managing the projectDeveloping strategies to support teamwork and learning
Skills it is said to developCreativity, cooperation, research and report-writingCritical thinking, problem solving, self-evaluation, longer retention of knowledge
Steps of each model
  • PjBL: (1) introduction and team planning, (2) initial research, (3) creating a prototype, (4) second research phase, (5) preparing the final presentation, (6) publishing the product.
  • PBL: (1) problem orientation, (2) organizing students for research, (3) independent and group investigation, (4) developing and presenting results, (5) analysing and evaluating the problem-solving process.

What both have in common: real-world issues, group work, and a teacher who acts as a facilitator.

How the study was done
  • Design: quasi-experiment with a control group (students were not randomly assigned, but whole classes were compared).
  • Place and time: SMA N 1 Temanggung, Central Java, January-February 2017.
  • Participants: 102 students from three science-track classes:
    • one class taught with PjBL
    • one class taught with PBL
    • one control class taught with the “5M” model.
  • Duration: six lessons on the respiratory system.
  • Measurement: all students took a pre-test (before) and a post-test (after) on creativity and critical thinking.
    • Creativity test: unusual uses, sensitivity to science problems, scientific imagination, creative problem solving, experimental design, and product design.
    • Critical thinking test: interpretation, analysis, inference, evaluation, and explanation.
  • Statistical analysis: comparison of before and after scores within each class, and comparison between the three classes.
What the students did

In the PjBL class

  • Project 1: students designed and tested a simple instrument to measure lung capacity, showing that it varies with different factors. They then wrote a report and presented it.
  • Project 2: students made anti-smoking posters, judged on originality, content, language, and accuracy.
  • Students planned their own schedules, gave each other feedback, and received advice from the teacher. They worked well in groups.

In the PBL class

  • Students received a problem about how cigarette content affects health, through articles and videos.
  • Experiment 1: they burned three types of cigarettes on white cotton (representing the lungs) and compared the yellow stains.
  • Experiment 2: they grew bean seeds in tobacco water and in plain water for four days and compared growth.
  • They then discussed a smoking-related disease, its causes, and how to prevent it, presented their findings, and reflected on the process. The teacher guided them but did not dictate how to design the experiments.
Main results
  1. All three classes improved in both creativity and critical thinking, including the control class.
  2. Creativity: the learning model made a real difference. The PjBL class obtained a clearly higher average score than both the PBL class and the control class, while PBL and the control class did not differ significantly.
  3. Critical thinking: there was no significant difference between PjBL, PBL, and the control class.
How the authors explain the results
  • Why PjBL improved creativity more:
    • Creativity involves observing problems, making guesses, testing them, and sharing results, which are the same steps the projects required.
    • Students were free to design their own products, so every group produced something different.
    • Making instruments and posters demanded originality, imagination, and design skills.
  • Why PBL scored lower in creativity: the authors suggest that PBL works better for long-term learning, so short-term tests may not show its full benefits.
  • Why critical thinking did not differ: the authors say many factors could be involved, including the way the lessons were carried out. In theory, PBL should be very good for critical thinking because it uses open problems and student-designed experiments, but this did not show up in the scores.
Conclusions
  1. Both PjBL and PBL appear to be linked to improvements in creativity and critical thinking.
  2. PjBL is better than PBL for creativity.
  3. There is no difference between PjBL and PBL for critical thinking.
  4. Teachers can use both models to make biology lessons richer, choosing according to their students and topic.
Limitations
  • The control class also improved, so the gains alone do not prove that PjBL or PBL caused them. Only PjBL’s advantage in creativity is clearly supported.
  • Small and limited study: one school, three classes, and only six lessons.
  • Classes started at different levels: creativity scores before the lessons were not the same in all classes, which may affect the comparison.

Article 3

PROJECT-BASED LEARNING CHARACTERISTIC AND THE EXPERIENCES WITH APPLICATION IN THE SCIENCE SUBJECTS

Kubiatko & Vaculová (2011) · Energy Education Science and Technology Part B, 3(1), 65-74

What is project based learning? (PBL)
  • PBL is a teaching method where students work on a project to solve a problem or answer a question.
  • It usually involves group work and ends with a final product (presentation, report, model...).
  • Students have an active role in their learning instead of just listening to the teacher.
  • It helps develop creativity, critical thinking, cooperation and problem-solving skills.
  • Projects are usually connected to real-life situations and students' interests, which makes learning more motivating.
Origins of PBL
  • Kilpatrick (1918): projects should be based on students' interests and connect different subjects. Students should plan, carry out and evaluate their own projects.
  • Vygotsky: learning happens through social interaction. Students can learn from each other and from the teacher.
Main characteristics
  • Driving question: a question or problem that guides the project.
  • Group work: students work together, share ideas and help each other.
  • Final product: something students create to show what they have learned.
  • Autonomy: students can make decisions about their learning.
  • Interdisciplinary: projects can involve different subjects.
  • Real-life connection: students apply what they learn to real situations.
  • Students can use different ways to present their knowledge (drawings, models, presentations...).
Types of projects (Morgan)
  • Project exercise: students use knowledge they already have. More teacher-centred.
  • Project component: bigger projects, often connected to real-life problems and different subjects. More student-centred.
  • Project orientation: the whole curriculum is based on projects, and students learn what they need during the process.
PBL in science education
  • Students investigate real scientific questions instead of just memorising information.
  • Everything starts with a driving question that guides the investigations.
  • Working in groups allows students to share different ideas and knowledge.
  • Technology can help students find information and present their results.
  • Students create a final product and can improve it after receiving feedback.
  • It also helps connect science with everyday life.
PBL vs. Problem-Based Learning
Project-Based Learningfocuses more on applying knowledge and creating a final product. Usually takes more time and requires planning and organising tasks.
Problem-Based Learningfocuses more on learning new knowledge through solving a problem. Usually shorter.
Teacher’s role
  • The teacher acts as a guide or facilitator, rather than just explaining everything.
  • They need to know students' interests and listen to their ideas.
  • They help students when they have difficulties but should not make all the decisions for them.
  • They also help solve conflicts and keep the project moving.
  • Finding a balance between giving students freedom and helping them is important.
Difficulties of PBL
  • Not enough time or resources.
  • Too many students in one classroom.
  • Teachers sometimes have little freedom to organise their lessons.
  • Lack of teacher training in this methodology.
  • Some students find it difficult to work independently or in groups.
Practical experience from the article

The authors explain an experience with future biology teachers. They had to prepare three projects:

  • Mineral waters
  • Human races
  • Birds of Slovakia

The projects were evaluated considering how interesting they were, the connection between subjects, the content, the organisation and the visual presentation.

Problems they found:

  • Students had difficulties connecting different subjects. Usually, one subject was much more important than the others.
  • Some presentations had too many colours and effects or very small text.
  • They used very few sources, mostly in their own language.
  • Sometimes the content was too easy or too difficult.
  • Some students were not really interested in the project and only did it because they had to.
Conclusion
  • PBL has many benefits, but it is still not used very often in schools.
  • One of the main reasons is that future teachers do not receive enough training about how to use it.
  • Teachers need to experience PBL themselves during their studies to be able to apply it in their future classrooms.

Article 6

Effects of project-based learning on students’ motivation and self-efficacy

Shin, M.-H. (2018). English Teaching, 73(1), 95-114 · DOI

The big idea

Shin (2018) wanted to know whether learning through a hands-on group project makes university students more motivated to learn English and more confident in their own abilities. Her idea is that students learn better when they do real, meaningful tasks together instead of just listening to a teacher.

Background
  • Project-based learning (PBL): students work in teams on a real-world task, make decisions together, solve problems, and get feedback along the way. The teacher acts more like a guide than a lecturer.
  • Technology: digital tools, such as making videos, fit well with group work. They give students real-life language practice and a reason to cooperate.
  • Motivation: the study uses Keller’s ARCS model. A motivating activity grabs Attention, feels Relevant, builds Confidence, and gives Satisfaction.
  • Self-efficacy: this is a person’s belief that they can do a task well (Bandura). People with high self-efficacy take on challenges and bounce back from failure. People with low self-efficacy tend to avoid hard tasks.
  • The link: earlier research suggests that the more motivated students are, the stronger their self-efficacy tends to be.
  • A known risk of teamwork: “Free riders,” students who let others do the work, can make group projects unfair.
Research questions
  1. How does PBL affect students’ motivation to learn English?
  2. How do students’ self-efficacy scores change (or relate to other things) after PBL?
  3. What do students think about PBL?
Method
Participants79 university students in a three-hour elective English course focused on speaking and listening. They were split into 13 teams (about 6 students each).
The projectEach team made a video about job interviews (“resume and cover letter video”). They chose interview questions, drew storyboards, acted out role-plays, and filmed a group video. Then each student made a personal self-introduction video. The work took 2-3 weeks.
Teacher’s roleThe teacher stayed in the background and was available by email or social media. Students presented their videos (on YouTube or a website), filled in feedback sheets for classmates, and did peer correction.
DataCollected from March to June 2017 using Likert-scale questionnaires (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). These covered motivation (based on ARCS), self-efficacy, and participation in the project. Teams were also graded on language use, cooperation, authenticity, and attitude.
AnalysisSPSS software, paired t-tests (before vs. after), and Pearson correlations.
Main findings

1. Motivation went up. Average scores rose from about 97 before the project to about 102 after, a statistically significant gain. By ARCS component:

  • Attention: significant increase
  • Relevance: significant increase (the biggest gain)
  • Confidence: no significant change
  • Satisfaction: no significant change

The author suggests confidence and satisfaction need more time to grow.

2. Self-efficacy matters

  • Self-efficacy was moderately correlated with project grades and with motivation after the project.
  • Students with higher self-efficacy did better on the project and were more motivated, which supports Bandura’s theory.

3. Students liked it

  • About 53% agreed they learned vocabulary and language use.
  • About 65% said the project helped them learn English actively.
  • About 75% enjoyed working cooperatively, and 72% said team relationships improved.
  • More than half found the project realistic and authentic.
  • About 69% said their attitude toward English changed.
  • Over 70% felt more confident and wanted PBL in future classes.
  • Only about 17% found the project troublesome.

Conclusions and teaching tips
  • PBL, especially with video, boosts attention and relevance, which helps students engage with English.
  • Teachers should notice that students with different self-efficacy levels react differently to the same project.
  • PBL is a good tool for improving relationships and teamwork in class.
Limitations (according to the author)
  • The sample (79 students) is too small to generalize.
  • There was only one project, which isn’t enough to measure activity reliably.
  • The teacher graded all projects, so the grades may be biased. Other graders are recommended.
  • Future studies should include other schools, majors, ages, and teacher or team-atmosphere variables.

This activity will be based on a narrative and four different challenges that students will complete in teams. The teams will be the same as those already established for the rest of the subject. Since the groups vary considerably in size, all the activities have been designed to adapt to different group sizes and ensure that everyone can participate.

Narrative for Primary students

Bad news: The school Wi-Fi has crashed! No tablets, no online games, no videos. The technician says the network is so damaged that only three teams can be reconnected. All the teams have 15 minutes to repair the network. The teams that create the strongest connections will earn more points, and the top three teams will get full Wi-Fi access first!

Scoring system (points)

The points of all the teams are added to the gym's whiteboard after each game.

Surprise bonus points

At the end of each activity, two teams will receive extra points for good teamwork.

The points will be given to the teams that work well together, help their teammates, communicate with each other, and make sure that everyone takes part in the activity.

The four missions

AMission 1: Signal Sprint (speed).

Each team must complete 10 relay runs to a cone placed 10 metres away and back. One player runs at a time and must high-five the next teammate before they can start.

To make it fair for teams of different sizes, some players may run more than once. However, every team must complete exactly 10 runs.

A teacher will count the completed runs and make sure that players go around the cone and wait for the high-five before starting. If a team does not follow these rules, that run will not count.

Teams are ranked according to their finishing order.

BMission 2: Network Sync (coordination).

Each team has 60 seconds to complete as many passes as possible. Players must pass the ball between their teammates, and everyone must touch the ball before the same player can receive it again.

Each successful pass counts as one point. If the ball drops, the team keeps its points and continues from where it stopped.

A teacher will count the passes and make sure that all team members participate and that no player receives the ball twice before everyone has touched it. Any pass that does not follow these rules will not count.

The team with the highest number of valid passes wins.

CMission 3: Steal the Bandwidth (Strategy and Agility).

Each team has a hoop with 5 tennis balls inside, representing their bandwidth units. Before the game starts, each team can choose where to place their hoop in the gym.

When the teacher says “START!”, teams decide their own strategy: some players can stay near their hoop to defend their balls, while others can attack other teams and steal their balls.

Players can only carry one ball at a time. To successfully steal a ball, they must take it from another team’s hoop and bring it back to their own hoop.

Defenders can protect their hoop by trying to touch one of the attacker’s feet. If an attacker is touched on the foot, they must immediately return to their own hoop without taking a ball. Once they reach their hoop, they can immediately leave again and continue attacking.

Players cannot push, grab or block other players. Defenders can only eliminate an attacker by touching their foot.

After 2 minutes, the teacher calls “STOP!” and everyone freezes. The balls in each hoop are counted.

The team with the most tennis balls wins the challenge.

DMission 4: Final Reset (teamwork under pressure).

Each team forms a human knot by standing in a circle and holding hands with different teammates.

One player from each team is chosen as the technician. Each technician moves to another team and must help that team untangle the human knot as quickly as possible.

The technician can give instructions and gently guide the players, but everyone must keep holding hands at all times. If anyone lets go, the team must stop, hold hands again and then continue.

The timer starts when the technician begins helping the team and stops when the human knot is completely untangled.

Teams are ranked according to how quickly they untangle their human knot. The fastest team receives 5 points, the second 4 points, the third 3 points, the fourth 2 points, and the fifth 1 point.

⏳ Timing and backup plan

Although the activity is designed to last approximately 15 minutes, we have prepared more missions than we may actually have time to complete. This gives us some flexibility depending on how quickly the class understands the instructions and completes each challenge. If the activities take longer than expected, we can simply leave out one of the missions. On the other hand, if everything runs faster than planned, we have an extra challenge ready as a backup, so students can remain active and engaged until the end of the session.

🏁 Closing

All the groups will gather in front of the whiteboard to see the current ranking. The teachers will then award surprise bonus points for teamwork, which may change the final classification. Once the points have been added, we will reveal which teams have made it onto the podium. The three top teams will receive a medal representing the Wi-Fi password, showing that they have successfully completed the mission.

🎒 Materials

  • 1 cone per team
  • 1 ball per team for Mission 2, plus 5 tennis balls per team for Mission 3
  • 1 hoop per team
  • A whistle, a timer and a whiteboard to write the classification

🥇 Prize

1st, 2nd and 3rd place Wi-Fi medals
